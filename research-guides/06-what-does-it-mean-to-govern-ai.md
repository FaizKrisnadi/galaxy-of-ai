# What does it mean to govern AI?

*There is no world regulator and never has been: governing AI is a patchwork of overlapping institutions, one ambitious European law that may not travel, firms improvising, and thin watchkeeping after things go wrong.*

## The short answer

Nobody is in charge of AI governance, if "in charge" means a single authority. A study in Global Studies Quarterly tracked 119 international institutions with a role in AI governance from 2014 to 2024 and found a "regime complex": many partly overlapping arrangements, little hierarchy, and a strong tilt towards Europe (Geith, Lundgren and Tallberg, 2026). The EU's AI Act is the most ambitious single rulebook, but two University of Amsterdam researchers argue its global influence is weaker than advertised. They find "informational disadvantages, reliance on external standard-setters, and enforcement delays", rules "watered down in the face of US competition", and, in the USA and China where cutting-edge AI is built, influence that "seems negligible so far" (Hoch and Mügge, 2026). Firms respond by coping: a study of EU companies describes "selective integration", bolting AI compliance onto older data-protection routines. After deployment the picture is thinnest of all. Of 307 AI incidents linked to US-listed companies, only 4 received incident-specific disclosure in securities filings (Chincholikar and Chawla, 2026). An engineering team built a working demonstration of post-market oversight for adaptive medical AI and labelled it "a proof-of-concept", not a clinical evaluation. One academic argument says thin and ordinary may be the right ambition anyway: govern AI like electricity, through control, understanding and resilience (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2025).

## What the sources can teach you

Start with the international picture, because it kills the simple story. [Geith, Lundgren and Tallberg](https://doi.org/10.1093/isagsq/ksag145) built a dataset of 119 international institutions involved in AI governance and tracked them across 2014 to 2024. Their findings are descriptive but pointed: the emerging "regime complex" lacks hierarchy, is geographically concentrated in Europe, and shows low functional differentiation, meaning institutions do similar things instead of dividing the work. They explain the pattern through state power, functional demands and earlier institutional choices, and warn that overlap plus regional concentration risks fragmented and inconsistent rules. One study, one decade, so treat it as a measured sketch rather than a final map.

The EU case shows what the biggest single rulebook can and cannot do. [Hoch and Mügge](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44206-026-00284-0) test the "Brussels Effect", the idea that EU rules become de facto world standards, and find four reasons for scepticism. Limited capacity: leverage is "undermined by informational disadvantages, reliance on external standard-setters, and enforcement delays", and firms customise by jurisdiction, so Meta withheld a model from the EU and Apple disabled features rather than comply worldwide. Pre-emptive concessions: rules were "watered down in the face of US competition", with Germany, France and Italy pushing back on strict rules for large language models in 2023 to protect Mistral and Aleph Alpha. Shallow harmonisation: vague principles leave the real detail to the EU's AI Office and standards bodies such as CEN/CENELEC and ISO/IEC, "whose own standards were delayed". Negligible influence where it matters: cutting-edge AI is concentrated in the USA and China, "the real litmus test", where EU influence "seems negligible so far"; the US rescinded its 2023 AI executive order in January 2025. The limits, they conclude, are "a wake-up call for crafting a more robust and independent European AI policy". On the firm side, [Restrepo Amariles and Baquero](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2026.106390) surveyed ten EU firms and interviewed four more: compliance means juggling the AI Act alongside data-protection and cybersecurity rules, aligning processes where the law allows and keeping them separate where it demands, with tools often rebuilt on older data-protection systems. "Selective integration" is their label for the result: adaptive but incomplete.

Then comes the part after deployment, most discussed and least practiced. [AEGIS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-03290-1), in npj Digital Medicine, is an engineering demonstration of what post-market oversight of adaptive medical AI could look like: a retraining module, a monitoring module, and a decision module outputting one of four verdicts (APPROVE, CONDITIONAL APPROVAL, CLINICAL REVIEW, REJECT) plus an independent ALARM signal. In the sepsis demo, a corrupted retraining iteration collapsed sensitivity, the share of true cases the model catches, from 0.723 to 0.428, triggering REJECT plus ALARM, exactly what the machinery is for. The authors insist it is "a proof-of-concept that exercises the governance mechanism; it is not a clinical evaluation". The gap it would fill is real: [Chincholikar and Chawla](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2026.1969275) matched 1,389 incidents from the AI Incident Database (2019 to 2026) to US-listed issuers, yielding 307 incidents across 21 companies, and found "Only 4 incidents (1.3%) received incident-specific disclosure, and 7 (2.3%) received any substantive disclosure", leaving a 97.7% "AI incident disclosure shadow". Substantive cases clustered on financial channels, such as a roughly $304 million Zillow write-down and GM's $478 million Cruise restructuring adjustment. The authors are careful: the denominator is media-derived, and the study is descriptive, not a legal-materiality or compliance determination. Public monitors cover part of the space. The OECD's [AI Incidents Monitor](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/incidents-monitor-aim) scans 150,000 news sources and over one million stories a day, and argues media reports are "only the tip of the iceberg" without a shared definition of an AI incident. The [AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/about/), begun by the Partnership on AI and now run by the Responsible AI Collaborative, indexes over a thousand real-world incidents, modelled on aviation accident databases and the CVE system for software vulnerabilities, and anyone can submit.

Two maps help you see the shape. The [MIT AI Governance Map](https://airisk.mit.edu/ai-governance) classifies more than 1,000 governance documents by risk, sector and scope, drawing on CSET's AGORA archive; the team cautions the classification is LLM-based, most documents are American, and patterns "should be taken as indicative". The OECD [AI Policy Navigator](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/international) is a "living repository" spanning more than 80 jurisdictions. Both lean US and EU, precisely where the Geith study finds governance already densest.

Finally, the framing argument. [Kapoor and Narayanan](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) argue AI is "normal technology", a claim that is "a description of current AI, a prediction about the foreseeable future of AI, and a prescription about how we should treat it". Their principles: control (people and organisations, not the technology itself, hold the levers), understanding capabilities (a persistent capability-reliability gap means exam-style benchmarks mislead), and diffusion, which "occurs over decades, not years". If that is right, systemic risks such as bias, inequality and eroding trust are "far more important than the catastrophic ones", and policy should treat "reducing uncertainty" as a first-rate goal and "resilience" as the overarching approach. This is an author argument from two Princeton AI researchers, not a measurement, but it explains why some people think the patchwork is a feature rather than a bug.

## Where researchers disagree

The regime-complex scholars see fragmentation itself as the problem and stop at describing it; Kapoor and Narayanan would call sector-specific, ordinary governance the point, and a unified AI regulator the wrong target. Hoch and Mügge quarrel with Brussels-effect optimism, and their remedy, a more independent European industrial policy, is itself contested. Method disputes run underneath. AEGIS works on retrospective data but has never run prospectively in a clinic; the disclosure study measures filings and explicitly does not claim anyone broke the law; MIT's own builders call its map indicative. Where one camp sees a governance vacuum, another sees governance that has simply not been counted.

## What remains uncertain

The AI Act's high-risk enforcement only began in August 2026, so no ex-post evidence exists yet, and the delayed CEN/CENELEC and ISO/IEC standards are not final. Restrepo's findings rest on fourteen firms in one jurisdiction. The disclosure study's denominator comes from media reports, so its percentages describe recorded incidents, not all corporate AI harm. AEGIS has no real-world deployment data and detects only one kind of drift, shifts in the input data, not every way a model can go wrong. Both maps underrepresent non-Western governance. And nobody here has measured which arrangement actually reduces harm; the link between institutions and outcomes is asserted, not shown.

## Readings to start with

1. [The Emerging Regime Complex for Artificial Intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1093/isagsq/ksag145) — the peer-reviewed descriptive study (Geith, Lundgren and Tallberg, 2026) that counted 119 institutions and found little hierarchy.
2. [EU Influence in Global AI Governance and its Limits](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44206-026-00284-0) — the peer-reviewed author argument (Hoch and Mügge, 2026) testing the Brussels Effect against the AI Act's early life.
3. [AI as Normal Technology](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) — the Kapoor and Narayanan essay (2025) arguing for ordinary, resilience-first governance.
4. [The AI incident disclosure shadow](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2026.1969275) — the peer-reviewed descriptive study (Chincholikar and Chawla, 2026) measuring how rarely AI incidents reach securities filings.
5. [AEGIS](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-03290-1) — the npj Digital Medicine proof-of-concept (Afdideh et al., 2026) for post-market governance of adaptive medical AI.
6. [Compliance under the EU AI Act](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2026.106390) — the peer-reviewed firm-level study (Restrepo Amariles and Baquero, 2026) behind "selective integration".
7. [MIT AI Governance Map](https://airisk.mit.edu/ai-governance) — a research tool classifying 1,000+ governance documents, with honest method caveats.
8. [The OECD AI Incidents Monitor](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/incidents-monitor-aim) — the OECD explainer on why shared incident reporting barely exists yet.

`society/trust — Trust`: whether people trust AI institutions depends partly on whether failures become visible at all.
