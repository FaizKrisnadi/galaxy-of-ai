# What do we know about AI's energy and water use?

*The electricity numbers are the best-anchored; the water numbers are mostly modelled, and almost no operator publishes verified operational figures.*

## The short answer

Electricity is the solid ground, and the numbers are smaller than headlines suggest. The International Energy Agency estimates data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, roughly 1.5 per cent of world electricity, and its Base Case scenario has that more than doubling to about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI "the most important driver" of the growth. These futures are scenarios, not forecasts; the IEA's 2030 cases spread from 670 to 1,260 TWh. In the United States, a congressionally requested Berkeley Lab report estimated 176 TWh in 2023, 4.4 per cent of national electricity, rising to a scenario range of 325 to 580 TWh by 2028. Water is thinner ground: the same report estimates US data centres directly consumed 66 billion litres in 2023, mostly for cooling, plus roughly 800 billion litres indirectly through electricity generation. In Southeast Asia, projections meet local taps: Ember projects Malaysia's data centre demand rising from about 9 to 68 TWh between 2024 and 2030, and Malaysian researchers report water requests from data centre projects already exceeding what reservoirs can supply. Research estimates fill what almost no operator's disclosure covers.

## What the sources can teach you

The global electricity picture is the most measured. The IEA's [Energy and AI report](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai) (April 2025) estimates data centres used 415 TWh in 2024, with the US at 45 per cent, China 25 per cent and Europe 15 per cent, and consumption growing around 12 per cent a year since 2017. Its Base Case reaches about 945 TWh by 2030, just under 3 per cent of world electricity; sensitivity cases range from 670 TWh (Headwinds) to 1,260 TWh (Lift-Off), widening to 700 to 1,700 TWh by 2035. Data centres account for less than 10 per cent of global electricity demand growth to 2030, but they "tend to concentrate in specific locations", which is why grid problems put around 20 per cent of projected additions at risk of delay. Southeast Asian demand should more than double by 2030, the report notes, helped by a hub straddling Singapore and Johor.

The United States has its own official estimate, built bottom-up. The [2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report.pdf) from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory models consumption from equipment shipments rather than meters, so its figures are modelled estimates. Its water section (report pages 55-58) is the best public accounting of US data centre water. Direct water use, mostly evaporative cooling, rose from 21.2 billion litres in 2014 to 66 billion litres in 2023, with hyperscale and colocation facilities accounting for 84 per cent of the 2023 total; hyperscale centres alone are projected to consume 60 to 124 billion litres by 2028. Indirect water, consumed at power plants to generate the electricity they draw, adds nearly 800 billion litres for 2023, at 4.52 litres per kilowatt-hour against 4.35 for US electricity overall. The report excludes power purchase agreements and on-site generation, so it reflects the local grid, not company claims.

Southeast Asia is where global curves become local politics. [Ember](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/from-ai-to-emissions-aligning-asean-digital-growth-with-energy-transition/), an independent energy think tank, projects Malaysia's data centre demand growing from 8.5 TWh in 2024 to 68 TWh by 2030, about 30 per cent of national power consumption, pushing data centre emissions from 5.9 to 40 million tonnes of CO2-equivalent on a grid still around 70 per cent fossil-fuelled. An [ISIS Malaysia policy brief](https://www.cert.isis.org.my/publications/ai-and-climate-considerations/) (June 2026) reports, from utility disclosures, that 7.5 gigawatts of data centre maximum demand had been secured by end-2025, 54 per cent of Peninsular Malaysia's contracted capacity, up from 43 per cent a year earlier; on a grid projected, per BMI research the brief cites, to stay more than 75 per cent fossil-fuelled until at least 2033, its authors conclude emissions are "potentially rising sevenfold". On water the brief cites the national regulator SPAN: about 808 million litres per day requested by data centre projects across three states, of which only under 20 per cent, around 142 million litres, can realistically be supplied, and government estimates put cooling needs at roughly 675 million litres a day. The brief's own finding, built on fifteen anonymised interviews, is that Malaysia's moratorium on non-AI data centres "cannot be practically enforced", because what a facility's tenants compute is unknown to operators and governments alike. An [ISEAS Perspective](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/articles-commentaries/iseas-perspective/2025-43-data-centres-energy-demand-and-sustainability-can-malaysia-strike-the-right-balance-by-sara-loo) (June 2025) documents the incentives behind the boom: a "Green Lane" cutting grid connection times from 36-48 months to 12, and efficiency guidelines that recommend rather than require.

Water stress becomes concrete in one reported place. A [Katadata long-form feature](https://katadata.co.id/data-center-batam/en) on Batam, the Indonesian island 45 minutes by ferry from Singapore, counts at least 18 data centres built or under construction, led by the NeutraDC-Nxera Kabil campus, whose cooling can draw up to 3 million litres of water a day. Batam has no major river and runs on six rain-fed reservoirs supplying about 382 million litres a day; in the 2020 drought its main reservoir fell 2.7 metres below the spillway, and 18 "stress areas" already ration water, some to two hours a day. Projected data centre demand of about 32 million litres a day by 2032 would be roughly 8.4 per cent of current supply. The reporting does not claim data centres caused these shortages: during a late-2024 crisis, officials blamed a pipe "technical issue", and every future figure is an attributed projection.

Research estimates fill the disclosure gap. Li, Yang, Islam and Ren's ["Making AI Less Thirsty"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271v1) paper (2023) estimates, in its abstract, that training GPT-3 in Microsoft's US data centres directly consumed about 700,000 litres of freshwater, enough to produce 370 BMW cars, and would have consumed three times as much in Microsoft's Asian data centres; these are modelled estimates, sensitive to location and cooling. A [peer-reviewed audit](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827721/) by Alex de Vries-Gao in Patterns (December 2025) sizes AI's 2025 water footprint at 312.5 to 764.6 billion litres and finds no company reports water use for AI workloads separately. ISIS Malaysia's conclusion that operational data are "generally not publicly disclosed by most companies" operating in Malaysia is the local version of the same finding.

## Where researchers disagree

The main disagreement is how alarmed to be. The IEA stresses that the data centre share of global electricity "remains limited", around 3 per cent even by 2030, while regional studies stress concentration: grids, reservoirs and ratepayers feel the megawatts locally, which is how Malaysia can have 54 per cent of Peninsular contracted capacity secured while the global share stays under 3 per cent. Both can be true; they answer different questions. Water accounting is also contested: de Vries-Gao argues the IEA's implied intensity of about 1 litre per kilowatt-hour is far below what company disclosures suggest, so the IEA likely underestimates indirect water, and Berkeley Lab's 4.52 litres supports that critique. LBNL adds a tradeoff: air cooling uses almost no water but more energy, so a low water figure is not automatically good. And efficiency cuts both ways: Ember relays an industry estimate that newer training methods are around 95 per cent more efficient, with the caveat that efficiency can spur more total demand.

## What remains uncertain

No comprehensive operational disclosure exists: no company in de Vries-Gao's audit separates AI from non-AI workloads, so every "AI water footprint" is a modelled estimate, not a measurement. The 2030 figures are scenarios whose spread (670 to 1,260 TWh) is the honest measure of uncertainty, and the IEA's key inputs are proprietary datasets from IDC, Omdia and SemiAnalysis that de Vries-Gao says cannot be easily validated. Water estimates are highly location-sensitive, as Li et al.'s Asian tripling shows; the 4.2 to 6.6 billion cubic metres of global AI water withdrawal projected for 2027, which ISIS Malaysia cites second-hand, belongs to a later version of that paper than the one whose abstract I read. ISIS Malaysia's interview findings are anonymised and not all corroborated. Batam's demand figures are projections, not records. Mandatory, standardised reporting per facility would settle much of this.

## Readings to start with

1. [Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai) — the IEA's scenario report, the reference point for global data centre electricity in 2024 and its 2030 spread.
2. [2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report.pdf) — the US Department of Energy's bottom-up estimate, with the best public water accounting (water section, report pages 55-58).
3. [From AI to Emissions](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/from-ai-to-emissions-aligning-asean-digital-growth-with-energy-transition/) — Ember's projections for six ASEAN countries' data centre power demand and emissions.
4. [AI and Climate Considerations](https://www.cert.isis.org.my/publications/ai-and-climate-considerations/) — ISIS Malaysia's policy brief on whether the boom fits Malaysia's climate goals, with the water-supply gap figures.
5. [Data Centres, Energy Demand and Sustainability](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/articles-commentaries/iseas-perspective/2025-43-data-centres-energy-demand-and-sustainability-can-malaysia-strike-the-right-balance-by-sara-loo) — an ISEAS Perspective on Malaysia's overcommitment and the limits of efficiency-metric policy.
6. [Making AI Less "Thirsty"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271v1) — the peer-reviewed paper whose abstract made AI's water footprint concrete, with its 700,000-litre GPT-3 estimate.
7. [The carbon and water footprints of data centers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827721/) — a peer-reviewed audit of what companies actually disclose about AI's footprints.
8. [A Digital Boom in a Thirsty City](https://katadata.co.id/data-center-batam/en) — reported long-form journalism on data centre expansion and water stress in Batam, Indonesia.
