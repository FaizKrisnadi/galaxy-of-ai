# Will AI change jobs — or change tasks within jobs?

*Indexes of what could change, payroll records of what has changed and surveys of what employers expect mostly point one way: AI exposes tasks far more than whole jobs, and where employment does move, it moves first for the youngest workers.*

## The short answer

The word "exposure" does a lot of work here, and it does not mean job loss. The ILO's global index finds one in four workers worldwide in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI, yet only 3.3% of global employment sits in the highest exposure band, mostly clerical roles, and the ILO itself stresses that exposure is a ceiling for what could change, not a count of jobs disappearing. What has actually been measured is narrower. US payroll data show no evidence of widespread, economy-wide displacement, but employment of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be if it had kept pace with less-exposed peers, a pattern the authors call descriptive, not causal. A Census Bureau working paper finds recent graduates of the most AI-exposed majors starting employment less often and earning about 13% less in their first full quarter. Employers tell surveys they expect cuts that are not yet visible in the data. In Southeast Asia the live question is readiness, not redundancy: small firms use little AI, skills are scarce, and workers report that literacy lowers anxiety. Tasks are changing fast; whole jobs are changing slowly; the entry rung of the ladder moves first.

## What the sources can teach you

Start with what could change. The ILO's [Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) (Working Paper 140, 2025) scores tasks rather than people: it draws on 29,753 tasks from Poland's detailed occupational classification, a survey of 1,640 workers and expert validation, then extends the scores to all international standard occupations. It puts 24% of world employment in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI, about one job in three in high-income countries but 11% in low-income ones. The gender pattern is sharp: 4.7% of women's employment worldwide falls in the highest exposure gradient against 2.4% of men's, widening to 9.6% versus 3.5% in high-income countries, because clerical work is where women are concentrated. Only 13 occupations make that top band, largely data entry clerks, typists, accounting and bookkeeping clerks and general office clerks. The authors are explicit that this is "exposure" and not "the actual impact on employment": the figures are an upper threshold if the technology were fully implemented, and in reality electricity, broadband, skills, cost and institutional reluctance hold adoption back. Most occupations keep tasks needing human input, so the "bundle of tasks" buffers against full automation, and the likeliest result is transformation rather than disappearance.

The same paper shows how shaky point estimates can be. The 2025 mean scores came out slightly lower than 2023's (0.29 versus 0.30 at occupation level), and the authors concede their earlier scores for some clerical tasks reflected "an overly optimistic assessment of full automation potential"; two years of real tool use taught them that meeting notes and scheduling still need people. Meanwhile exposure rose for strongly digitised occupations such as web and media developers and software-related roles. Their own historical analogy is bank tellers after ATMs: the machine took the cash counting, and the banks opened more branches. An index tells you where pressure lands, not what happens next.

What has happened, in one country, comes from payroll records. [Canaries in the Coal Mine?](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/) (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised August 2026) uses ADP payroll data covering millions of US workers through June 2026 and states six facts: no evidence of widespread, economy-wide displacement; employment of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed occupations 19% below its kept-pace counterfactual, with no comparable gap for experienced workers; a gap that has widened since August 2025; adjustment through reduced hiring rather than layoffs; declines concentrated where AI substitutes for human tasks while complementary use leaves employment flat or rising; and adjustment through employment rather than base pay. In level terms, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11% between November 2022 and June 2026 while the same age group in the three least exposed quintiles grew about 10%. The authors' own words matter: these are "early, descriptive indicators — canaries in the coal mine — rather than causal estimates", and the library's standing note agrees. The paper names its weak spots: the pattern attenuates when controlling for education, some divergent trends predate generative AI around the pandemic, and the effect is more pronounced in the ADP sample than in national survey benchmarks.

A second US dataset tells a matching story about the newest workers. The Census Bureau working paper [Graduating into Disruption](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html) (Orr, Tucker and Warren, September 2026) compares outcomes of college majors by their AI exposure. Graduates of the most AI-exposed decile of majors saw their likelihood of initial employment fall by five percentage points and full-quarter initial earnings fall by 13%, which the authors size as roughly the penalty of graduating into a major recession. About half the earnings decline is lower pay within industries; the other half is movement toward lower-wage sectors like restaurants and retail. Effects fade with distance from graduation but stay substantial. This is a regression-adjusted association, and the library's record notes AI cannot be isolated cleanly in it; still, two independent datasets now agree that the entry point moves first.

What firms say is a third stream. The [AI Index Report 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) economy chapter, a compilation of third-party sources rather than original research, reports AI's labor market effects "concentrated in hiring pipelines and the youngest workers in exposed occupations. Employment for software developers ages 22 to 25 has fallen nearly 20% from 2024" (as reported by HAI, drawing on the Stanford study above). The same chapter reports that one-third of surveyed organizations expect AI to reduce their workforce in the coming year, "even though large-scale job losses have not yet shown up in overall employment data". Keep the streams separate: what could be exposed (the index), what has happened (payroll), and what employers expect (surveys). Only the first two involve anyone counting anything.

Outside the United States the picture is mostly about readiness. A [Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung volume chapter](https://www.kas.de/documents/287213/33744488/The+Experiences+and+Extent+of+Adopting+AI+among+SMEs+in+Southeast+Asia.pdf) by John Paul Flaminiano notes that small and medium enterprises are over 99% of all firms in Southeast Asia and lists the common adoption barriers: "a shortage of skilled workers, insufficient talent development programs, inadequate digital infrastructure, and high financial costs". Readiness scores it compiles run from Singapore's 70.1 down to the Philippines' 35.7, and it warns the gap risks "a digital divide and increased digital inequality"; within its Philippine evidence, manufacturing SMEs' AI use has been "limited to chatbots". A [peer-reviewed Thai study](https://ph02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/SciTechAsia/article/view/256785) surveyed 318 Thai workers and found AI literacy significantly lowers anxiety (a standardised coefficient of -0.892), while perceived ease of use strongly drives acceptance: the psychological barrier arrives before the technical one. And a [2024 Chulalongkorn master's thesis](https://digital.car.chula.ac.th/chulaetd/74149) on China, using provincial panel data for 2006 to 2022, associates AI adoption with lower employment among low- and medium-skilled workers, higher wages, and little effect on high-skill occupations, a pattern economists call skill-biased technological change: technology favouring the skilled. That is abstract-level evidence from one country, read at record level, not a global law.

## Where researchers disagree

The main dispute is over which kind of evidence answers the question. Task-exposure indexes measure what could be automated; payroll studies measure what has happened in one country's firms; employer surveys measure what firms expect. They disagree by construction. The ILO's own revision cut its 2023 clerical scores after real-world experience, which suggests exposure indexes carry error bars nobody prints. The Ada Lovelace Institute's [Measuring Up](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/policy-briefing/measuring-up/) briefing makes the sharper critique: task-automation methods that assume all tasks weigh equally and "use large language models (LLMs) to assess LLM capabilities" are "becoming standard - and automated - government practice". The ILO's own methods note admits all tasks are weighted equally because occupational classifications lack task-importance data. There is also a framing fight: exposure cuts both ways. The ILO's lowest gradient is close to the old augmentation idea, and the Stanford payroll study finds employment flat or rising precisely where AI complements workers, so an exposed occupation can end up automated, transformed, or enlarged, and current data cannot always say which in advance. Finally, geographies disagree: effects measured on US payroll records, Chinese provincial panels or 318 Thai survey respondents do not add up to a global claim, and the strongest measured effects so far concern young workers in the United States.

## What remains uncertain

Causation is the big one: the Stanford authors call their facts descriptive, the Census authors cannot isolate AI cleanly, and the Chulalongkorn thesis is read at abstract level here. Whether the young-worker gap persists as cohorts age, or reflects postponed entry rather than lost careers, is unknown. The ILO's estimates are a static snapshot from early 2025 that counts no new tasks or jobs created, and no source here can say what those are. Southeast Asian evidence rests on small or qualitative samples: a single-author chapter's literature synthesis, focus-group work belonging to a separate chapter of the same volume, and a 318-person cross-sectional survey with self-reported adoption. Employer expectations could materialise or not; HAI's own compilation says large-scale losses are not yet visible. Wage effects beyond entry-level, job quality for those who stay, and the fate of clerical work in low-income countries all lack measured answers in these sources.

## Readings to start with

1. [Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) — the ILO working paper (2025) that quantifies task exposure worldwide and insists, in its own conclusions, that exposure is not job loss.
2. [Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/) — the Stanford Digital Economy Lab working paper measuring hiring effects on US payroll data, with its caveats stated in the abstract.
3. [Graduating into Disruption: Labor Market Outcomes for AI-Exposed College Majors](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html) — a US Census Bureau working paper on graduates entering a changed market.
4. [AI Index Report 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) — Stanford HAI's annual compilation; its economy chapter aggregates what firms report and expect, clearly a third-party compilation.
5. [The Experiences and Extent of Adopting AI among SMEs in Southeast Asia](https://www.kas.de/documents/287213/33744488/The+Experiences+and+Extent+of+Adopting+AI+among+SMEs+in+Southeast+Asia.pdf) — a KAS policy volume chapter on why small firms in the region adopt slowly.
6. [Assessing AI Literacy and Readiness in Thailand's Workforce](https://ph02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/SciTechAsia/article/view/256785) — a peer-reviewed journal article linking AI literacy, anxiety and acceptance among 318 Thai workers.
7. [The impact of artificial intelligence on China's labor market](https://digital.car.chula.ac.th/chulaetd/74149) — a 2024 Chulalongkorn University master's thesis using Chinese provincial panel data; abstract-level evidence, clearly labelled as such.

## Where this sits in the Galaxy

`economics/jobs — Jobs and work`: the guide's home ground, where exposure, hiring and transformation meet.

`economics/productivity — Productivity`: task-level gains are the mirror image of task-level exposure, and both decide whether jobs transform or shrink.

`economics/evidence — Evidence and measurement`: this guide is mostly about what different instruments (indexes, payroll data, surveys) can and cannot measure.

`society/skills — Skills and learning`: literacy lowers anxiety, and skills gaps are the top adoption barrier for Southeast Asian small firms.

`society/attitudes — Attitudes and expectations`: employers' expectations run ahead of measured outcomes, and workers' feelings shape adoption before capability does.
