# Who owns the data used to develop AI?

*Less than anyone, and more than you can see: the law mostly decides who owns AI outputs rather than the data behind them, most frontier training corpora are closed and undisclosed, and the open datasets we can actually inspect exist because public institutions built them deliberately.*

## The short answer

"Owning training data" turns out to be three different questions. First, who owns what AI produces: the U.S. Copyright Office answered in January 2025 that copyright questions about AI "can be resolved pursuant to existing law", that purely AI-generated material gets no copyright, and that prompts alone do not make you an author. Whether AI companies may train on copyrighted works in the first place is a different question, which the Office explicitly deferred to a later report (U.S. Copyright Office, 2025). Second, who holds the corpora: for frontier models, mostly private firms, and their contents are largely undisclosed, so nobody outside can check what is in them. But open counter-examples prove it can be otherwise: the OLMo project released its 2,668-billion-token Dolma dataset under a permissive licence, AI Singapore trains on a documented 200-billion-token mix dominated by Southeast Asian languages, and AI4Bharat assembled 230 million translation pairs covering all 22 scheduled Indian languages. Third, who gets to participate: the GovAI institute argues the "global AI majority" faces "Anglocentric data sources", and the African Union warns data are "usually sourced from developed countries". So ownership is thickest where data is closed, thinnest where it is open, and the participation question is only beginning to be answered.

## What the sources can teach you

Begin with the law as written, because it is narrower than the debate suggests. The [U.S. Copyright Office's Part 2 report on copyrightability](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf) (January 2025) grew out of a public consultation that drew over 10,000 comments from all 50 states and 67 countries. Its executive summary holds that questions of copyright in AI outputs "can be resolved pursuant to existing law, without the need for legislative change"; that using AI as an assistive tool does not affect protection; that "copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material"; that whether human contributions suffice "must be analyzed on a case-by-case basis"; and that "based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone provide sufficient control". The Office also declined to create new protection: "the case has not been made for additional copyright or sui generis protection for AI-generated content". Crucially for the ownership question, this report does not answer it: the Office stated that "a subsequent part" would address "the training of AI models on copyrighted works, licensing considerations, and allocation of any liability". What a person can own in an AI output is settled policy in the US; what a company may own, or owe, for the data it trained on is not.

Now look at the datasets that exist and are documented, because they show both what openness looks like and what it costs. The [OLMo paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00838), from the non-profit Allen Institute for AI (2024), trained a competitive 7-billion-parameter model, scoring 69.3 on its benchmark average against Llama 2's 70.5, while releasing the whole apparatus: the Dolma pretraining corpus of 2,668 billion tokens, over 500 intermediate checkpoints, and the complete training logs, all under Apache 2.0, a licence permitting nearly any use including commercial. The authors state their motive plainly: prior releases "have only released model weights and inference code", and they "believe it is essential for the research community to have access to powerful, truly open LMs". Their limitations section is equally honest: the training data is English-only, and like most web-scale corpora it likely contains "toxic language, personal information, and copyrighted text" despite filtering. Building open data for overlooked languages is harder still. AI4Bharat's [IndicTrans2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16307) (published in TMLR, 2023) describes assembling the Bharat Parallel Corpus Collection: 230.5 million translation pairs after deduplication, of which 126 million were newly mined and only 644,000 were translated by humans for the corpus. Getting there meant throwing away about 75 percent of the existing public data as badly aligned (Meta's NLLB contribution shrank from 448.1 million pairs to roughly 85 million), and mining just 0.4 million usable pairs for Urdu out of 76.2 million monolingual sentences. Across the whole corpus, 2.2 million pairs are gold, human-annotated by professional translators; the rest is mined and filtered machine-scale data. The models and data were released under permissive licences, which the authors chose deliberately: Meta's NLLB, they note, carries a non-commercial licence that blocks exactly the reuse open data is meant to enable.

AI Singapore's contribution shows public data programmes as a genre. The [SEA-LION paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05747) (2025) documents continual pre-training, continued training of an existing model on new data, on a 200-billion-token mix: 55 percent Southeast Asian languages, 25 percent English, 20 percent code. That mix lifted the 8B model's SEA-HELM benchmark score by 6.05 points over its Llama base, and the 9B model's by 7.19, and the released models carry a fully open MIT licence. The underlying data programme, [SEALD](https://aisingapore.org/aiproducts/southeast-asian-languages-in-one-network-data-seald), is a collaboration between AI Singapore and Google Research covering nine Southeast Asian languages in text and speech, with a promise that "AISG and Google will release the datasets and output from Project SEALD in open-source". Note what the page does not say: no dataset sizes, no licence names, no release dates. It is programme information, not a documented dataset, so treat it as intent. And the provider-claim category matters even for the good actors: Ai2's [OLMo 3 announcement](https://allenai.org/blog/olmo3) (November 2025) describes a new ~9.3-trillion-token Dolma 3 corpus, released with code, weights and checkpoints "without any license restrictions", and states that Ai2 follows "established web standards in collecting training data" and does "not collect from sites that explicitly disallow it, including paywalled content". Every performance and openness claim there is Ai2's own, in a blog post selling its approach against named "open-weight" rivals that release weights but not data. None of it has been independently verified.

Against the documented few stands the undocumented many. Nothing in this library can tell you what is inside the training corpora of the major closed models, because their operators do not publish compositions, sources or licence statuses. What can be inferred is limited but real: the OLMo paper shows what scale looks like when disclosed; the USCO report shows governments assume the training question is live enough to need its own report; and the absence of disclosure is itself a fact a reader can hold. The participation side fills in why that absence matters. A [GovAI whitepaper](https://governance.ai/research-paper/voice-and-access-in-ai-global-ai-majority-participation-in-artificial-intelligence-development-and-governance) (October 2024, an institute argument) names the barriers facing what it calls the "global AI majority": "limited access to digital and compute infrastructure, power concentration in AI development, Anglocentric data sources, and skewed talent distributions", and proposes three remedies, interest alignment, participatory architecture and safety assurance, including national data infrastructures so more countries can build with their own languages and records. The [African Union's Continental AI Strategy](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf) makes the same critique from an official podium: AI systems may amplify bias because "data are usually sourced from developed countries and from non-diverse and inclusive developers' teams", and its sixth action area calls for "the availability of high-quality and diverse datasets for AI", with data "in an open format or through regulatory sandboxes". These are arguments and strategy text, not measurements, but they identify exactly the layer the open datasets above are built to supply.

## Where researchers disagree

On copyright, the disagreement was surveyed rather than resolved: the Copyright Office reports that the "vast majority" of commenters agreed existing law is adequate and wholly AI-generated material is unprotectable, but commenters split on how much human contribution counts, and on policy some argued that protecting AI outputs would encourage creation while others warned it would "undermine incentives for humans to create". On openness, Ai2's "fully open" definition (data, code and weights public) is itself a competitive framing aimed at "open-weight" rivals, and nothing in these sources settles where the line should sit; SEALD's sponsors promise openness, IndicTrans2's authors show openness chosen over a restrictive alternative, and the closed frontier stays closed. The USCO's training report is the missing arbiter: the library holds no source on it at all, so this guide can describe the fight but not its outcome.

## What remains uncertain

The largest uncertainty is the one the Copyright Office deferred: whether training on copyrighted works is lawful in the US, and how other jurisdictions treat it. This library has exactly one copyright source, so there is no EU text-and-data-mining exception text to compare, and no source at all on the licensing market that might intermediate between rights-holders and developers. For the open datasets: OLMo's Dolma is English-only; SEA-LION's models were evaluated only at 8B and 9B scale, and its Indonesian scores dropped slightly after alignment, a stated trade-off; SEALD's sizes, licence and dates do not exist publicly yet; IndicTrans2's benchmark test sets are mostly English-original, a limitation its own authors state. OLMo 3's claims are provider statements, not audited. And nothing here measures the composition of closed frontier corpora; the only honest statement is that it is unknown, and unknown by design.

## Readings to start with

1. [Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf) — the U.S. Copyright Office's official report (2025) on who owns AI outputs; the training question is deferred to a later part.
2. [OLMo: Accelerating the Science of Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00838) — the Ai2 team's open paper (2024) documenting a fully open model, the 2,668-billion-token Dolma dataset and every training artifact.
3. [OLMo 3](https://allenai.org/blog/olmo3) — Ai2's provider blog post (2025) claiming full-flow openness for a ~9.3-trillion-token corpus, with unverified performance claims.
4. [SEA-LION: Southeast Asian Languages in One Network](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05747) — the AI Singapore paper (2025) behind a documented 200-billion-token, 55 percent Southeast Asian training mix and MIT-licensed models.
5. [Project SEALD](https://aisingapore.org/aiproducts/southeast-asian-languages-in-one-network-data-seald) — the AI Singapore and Google Research programme page (living) for nine-language datasets promised as open source.
6. [IndicTrans2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16307) — the peer-reviewed AI4Bharat paper (TMLR, 2023) on building the 230-million-pair BPCC corpus for all 22 scheduled Indian languages, under permissive licences.
7. [Voice and Access in AI](https://governance.ai/research-paper/voice-and-access-in-ai-global-ai-majority-participation-in-artificial-intelligence-development-and-governance) — the GovAI whitepaper (2024) on participation barriers, including "Anglocentric data sources", and three governance strategies.
8. [Continental AI Strategy](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf) — the African Union's 2024 strategy, whose executive summary names non-diverse data sourcing as a core risk.
