# How do countries differ in their approaches to AI?

*Countries are not standing on one ladder with the leaders at the top. Each government is optimising for something different: speed, rules, market power, public good, administrative reality, constitutional rights, or development, and the differences show up in what they actually do.*

## The short answer

Ask what each approach is for, and the map becomes readable. China optimises for capability and speed: one industry research firm analysing 857 model releases found 94.9 percent shipped with no published safety result at all, while the promised comprehensive AI law was shelved (SemiAnalysis, 2026). The European Union optimises for rules, though two political scientists find its influence "seems negligible so far" where frontier AI is actually built, and Europe's other bet, public supercomputing for startups, is a programme description rather than a measured success (Hoch and Mügge, 2026; EuroHPC JU). The United States optimises for market leadership, with critics arguing the AI industry converts geopolitical rivalry into government patronage (AI Now Institute, 2025). Singapore refreshes a state strategy for public benefit, over S$1 billion committed to research and talent through 2030. Vietnam wrote AI into law as "essential national infrastructure" (Tech for Good Institute, 2026). Indonesia shows the harder case: a peer-reviewed study found central ministries rejecting local AI proposals while provinces struggle with basic e-government (Wadipalapa et al., 2024). India grounds principles in constitutional rights; Japan consolidated soft-law guidelines after nearly 4,000 public comments. The African Union frames everything as people-centric development, and Latin America's official index measures the gap: 6.6 percent of world GDP, 8.8 percent of its population, 1.12 percent of AI investment (ECLAC and CENIA, 2025).

## What the sources can teach you

Start with China, because it breaks the assumption that more rules mean slower AI. [SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/beijing-will-not-pace-the-frontier), a subscription industry research firm (this is their analysis, not peer-reviewed work), published "Beijing Will Not Pace the Frontier" in October 2026 arguing China's regime is "speed-based, not safety-based". The safety governance framework issued by TC260, China's standards body, opened by making innovation and development the first priority, and the comprehensive AI Law promised in China's legislative plans was shelved in 2025. Regulation targets applications, content and outputs: labelling rules, protections for minors, inspections. Nothing triggers frontier-risk duties based on training compute or capability. The firm compiled its own dataset of 857 releases from nine leading Chinese developers between 2021 and September 2026: 31 releases (3.6 percent) ever had a published safety result, 9 (1.1 percent) at or before launch, and 813 (94.9 percent) had none. In a second dataset of 102 expert and official texts, frontier or loss-of-control risk appears in 86 percent of technical scientists' writings but only 22 percent of legal scholars'. Those datasets are the firm's own compilation and have not been independently audited, but the pattern they describe, strict on content, loose at the frontier, is the opposite of Europe's.

Europe chose rules first, and its own scholars document the price. [Hoch and Mügge](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44206-026-00284-0), two University of Amsterdam political scientists writing in the peer-reviewed journal Digital Society, argue the AI Act has run into four limits, including firms customising models by jurisdiction (Meta withheld a model from the EU; Apple disabled features), rules "watered down in the face of US competition", and influence that "seems negligible so far" in the USA and China. Guide 6 covers their argument in depth; here it marks one pole of the map, a jurisdiction that optimises for trustworthy rules rather than frontier capability. Europe's counter-bet on capability runs through public infrastructure: the [EuroHPC Joint Undertaking](https://www.eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/ai-factories_en) reports 19 "AI Factories" and 13 "AI Factory Antennas" built around AI-optimised supercomputers, offering "free, customised support to SMEs and Startups". Note the caveat: this is an official programme description, not evidence of who used the factories or with what results.

The United States is the market-led pole. There is no comprehensive federal AI statute in these sources; what a reader finds instead is [AI Now Institute's 2025 Landscape Report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/research/ai-now-2025-landscape-report), which is advocacy from a critical research institute and should be read as such. Its argument is that the US-China "arms race" framing is itself an industry asset: it "affords them patronage not just from their own government, but potentially from the many other nation-states" and insulates firms from regulatory friction. Hoch and Mügge, writing from a very different standpoint, describe the same tilt from the official record: the 2023 Biden executive order on AI was rescinded in January 2025, and a July 2025 executive order demanded that American AI "technologies, standards, and governance models" be adopted worldwide. Whatever one thinks of AI Now's politics, the direction of travel in its account and in the orders is the same: industrial policy plus deregulation, in support of firms already at the frontier.

Singapore and Vietnam show the state-strategy genre in two moods. Singapore's [Ministry of Digital Development and Information](https://www.mddi.gov.sg/newsroom/update-to-singapore-s-national-ai-strategy--refreshed-priorities-to-harness-ai-for-the-public-good-factsheet/) published a factsheet in May 2026 refreshing the National AI Strategy around ten priorities, from sectoral "AI Missions" to a compute priority: "advance resource-efficient AI and enable sufficient access to compute in line with Singapore's sustainability goals". The document notes compute access improved after the acute constraints of 2023 but "future supply dynamics remain uncertain", and names green data centres, a second data-centre call for applications, and the ASPIRE 2B supercomputer, backed by over S$1 billion committed to public AI research and talent from 2025 to 2030. This is an official strategy statement; no capacity figures appear, so treat it as intent. Vietnam's entry is [a country spotlight](https://techforgoodinstitute.org/insights/country-spotlights/vietnams-ai-strategy-aligning-aspirations-with-adaptive-governance) by Dr Vu Minh Khuong of the Lee Kuan Yew School, published by the Tech for Good Institute (the author's views, not institute doctrine). It reports that Vietnam's AI Law, passed in December 2025 and effective March 2026, mirrors the EU's four-tier risk classification, treats AI as "essential national infrastructure", and puts a National AI Committee under the Prime Minister. The author's caution is the interesting part: an "excessively control-heavy approach" could limit collaboration and trap AI "at the pilot stage", because Vietnam's real constraints are weak data sharing and slow procurement, not ambition.

Indonesia is the peer-checked reality check. [Wadipalapa and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/18681034231226393), all researchers at Indonesia's National Research and Innovation Agency, published in the Journal of Current Southeast Asian Affairs a study built on interviews with 25 officials in Jakarta, Central Java and East Java between June and September 2023. Their findings describe an ambitious national AI strategy colliding with a decentralised state: the Ministry of Communication and Information "rejected numerous AI innovation proposals from local governments" with no published assessment protocol; an expert staffer reported over half of East Java's areas still failing the basic e-government mandate; officials in two provinces said they were barely informed the national strategy existed; and governors promoted AI ahead of the 2024 elections, with one flagship complaint app found to rely mainly on human operators. Three provinces, one point in time, and the authors' interpretation of interviewees' statements, so not a nationwide verdict, but a demonstration that a national approach is also an administrative fact, not just a document.

The remaining sources show what principles look like when they are anchored somewhere specific. India's NITI Aayog, a government think tank, issued [Responsible AI: Approach Document for India, Part 1](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2021-02/Responsible-AI-22022021.pdf) in 2021, deriving principles "grounded in the fundamental rights afforded to citizens by the Constitution"; the seven principles, from safety and reliability to the protection of positive human values, rest on constitutional articles on equality, non-discrimination, life and privacy. The document itself credits a World Economic Forum centre as knowledge partner and notes "Legal inputs enabled by Microsoft", corporate involvement acknowledged on its own pages. Japan took the soft-law route: [METI and the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications](https://www.meti.go.jp/english/press/2024/0419_002.html) consolidated three older guidelines into AI Guidelines for Business in April 2024 after a public comment period, a government-release-level account of guidance for business actors rather than statute. The [African Union's Continental AI Strategy](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf) (July 2024) is the development-first document: a "people-centric, development-oriented and inclusive approach" that names an "AI divide between and within countries" and warns that "data are usually sourced from developed countries and from non-diverse and inclusive developers' teams". Latin America's contribution is not a strategy but a measurement: the [ILIA 2025 executive summary](https://www.cepal.org/en/publications/84471-latin-american-artificial-intelligence-index-ilia-2025-executive-summary), an index of 19 countries by the UN's ECLAC and Chile's CENIA, finds the region holds 6.6 percent of global GDP and 8.8 percent of its population yet attracts 1.12 percent of global AI investment, and "invests four times less in AI than would be expected given its weight in the global economy".

## Where researchers disagree

The sharpest dispute is what China's regime actually is. SemiAnalysis says safety-forward language masks speed-first practice; a reader should hold that against the firm's commercial position as a subscription research outfit and its self-compiled datasets. On Europe, Hoch and Mügge are deliberately contrarian against Brussels-effect optimism, and their remedy, a more independent European industrial policy, is itself contested. A third disagreement runs between regulate-first and build-first framings. The African Union's first action area is "an appropriate AI governance system and regulations"; ILIA's coordinators, by contrast, worry their region "advances slowly and timidly, often prioritizing regulatory debates that may hinder the development of technology". Vietnam's spotlight author splits the difference, warning that control-heavy governance can strangle the collaboration a latecomer needs. Indonesia complicates every camp: all of them assume a state that can execute, and Wadipalapa's interviews show central gatekeeping and local capacity gaps undermining strategy on the ground.

## What remains uncertain

No source here compares approaches on a common metric; sorting them by what they optimise for is this guide's framing, not a finding. SemiAnalysis's two datasets cannot be checked from outside the paywall; its full text is subscription-only. Wadipalapa's study covers three provinces in 2023 and reports interviewees' claims, not verified performance. Singapore's factsheet commits money and priorities but shows no delivered compute. EuroHPC's page counts factories, not users. The Vietnamese account is one author's assessment published by an institute. India's document is from 2021, before later Indian AI governance developments, and states principles, not law. Japan's entry is press-release level; the guidelines document itself was not read, and a 2025 update exists outside the catalogue. The AU strategy is aspiration without implementation evidence in the sections read. ILIA's investment comparisons rest on index methodology in an unread 184-page report. China, the US and the EU each lack a comprehensive official document in this library, so their portraits lean on think-tank and firm analysis.

## Where this sits in the Galaxy

`politics/national — National AI politics`: Singapore, Vietnam, India, Indonesia and the AU show governments writing strategy into law, principles or constitutions.

`politics/international — International politics`: the US-China rivalry frames both China's speed-first regime and, critics argue, America's patronage of its own firms.

`society/participation — Public participation`: Japan's 3,947 public comments and India's consultations are the visible cases of publics being asked.

`governance/rules — Rules and regulation`: the EU's AI Act and its documented limits are the reference case; guide 6 follows them in detail.

`infrastructure/cloud — Compute and cloud`: Singapore's compute priority and Europe's AI Factories show states treating compute as a public asset.

`economics/investment — Investment and finance`: ILIA's 1.12 percent figure is the measured baseline for who is actually funding AI.
