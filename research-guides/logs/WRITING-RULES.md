# Reading-guide rules (shared by all writers)

## What a guide is
A single markdown file answering one clear question with the Galaxy of AI library's own sources. The reader is curious, smart, and has no technical background — think a well-read 15-year-old or an interested adult. Nothing here is the website or the catalogue; this is a self-contained set of essays.

## Voice
- Plain English. Short sentences are fine. Explain any term you must use, the first time.
- Sound like a person who actually read the sources, not like a summary machine. Vary sentence openings. No "In today's rapidly evolving landscape". No "delve". No "It's important to note". No bullet-point dumps in the explanation sections — connected prose.
- Use concrete images and specific numbers sparingly but exactly. Never invent or round-up a number. Every figure must come from a source you read (or from the library's verified record of it), and you must be able to say which source it came from.
- No em-dash-heavyAI cadence. Prefer ordinary punctuation.

## Labelling claims (essential)
Always make the epistemic status visible in the flow of the sentence:
- measured outcome (a study counted something) — say who measured, on what group/period.
- forecast / scenario (a modeller projected something) — never present as fact.
- company claim (the maker of a product describing its own product) — say so.
- author argument / advocacy (an institute or essayist's position) — say so.
- plausible concern / open question — say so.
Also: findings about one country, one firm, or one occupation are not global conclusions. Distinguish "exposure" from "job loss", "usage" from "ownership", "correlation" from "cause".

## Required structure (in this order)
1. `# <The question, as a title>`
2. One-sentence italic framing line under the title.
3. `## The short answer` — 150–250 words a curious 15-year-old can follow.
4. `## What the sources can teach you` — 3–6 short prose paragraphs, each anchored to specific sources (name + link on first mention). This is the body; make it interesting and specific.
5. `## Where researchers disagree` — genuine tensions: method disputes, different framings, different geographies. If sources agree, say what they agree on and move on.
6. `## What remains uncertain` — honest limits: what cannot be inferred from these sources, what data does not exist, what would need checking.
7. `## Readings to start with` — 5–8 items, each: `n. [Title](direct URL) — one sentence on why it is useful, naming the kind of source it is (e.g. "IEA scenario report", "company blog post", "peer-reviewed study").` No bare link lists.
8. `## Where this sits in the Galaxy` — 3–6 related Galaxy topics, each with one sentence in ordinary language on how it connects. Use topic names like `how/inference — Inference & everyday use` as plain text (no website links; the guides are standalone).
9. `## Notes on the sources` — a compact honesty section: for each source used, what portion you actually read (e.g. "executive summary + pp. 49–54 of the PDF", "abstract only", "publisher page; full text paywalled") and anything inaccessible or unclear. State plainly: "I read X; I did not read Y."

## Mechanics
- Direct links only. Use the catalogue's URL unless it is dead — if a link 404s or is gated beyond its abstract, say so in Notes on the sources and keep the link (the reader may have access).
- Dates: sources carry their own dates; give the year for anything time-sensitive.
- Do not fabricate page numbers. Only cite a page/section if you actually saw it.
- Quote sparingly (short phrases, in quotation marks, attributed). Long quotations are not wanted.
- Word budget: roughly 900–1,400 words per guide. Substance over length.
- File name and location are assigned to you. Write nothing outside `research-guides/`.
- Write the guide in one pass, then re-read it for: overstatement ("proves" for a single study), broken flow, repeated phrases, and jargon.
