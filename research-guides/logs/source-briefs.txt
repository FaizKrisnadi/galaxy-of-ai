--- speculative-decoding-2023
title: Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding
url: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192
authors: Yaniv Leviathan, Matan Kalman, Yossi Matias | date: 2022-11-30 | inst: Google Research
topics: how/inference, how/models, economics/productivity
verification: abstract read 2026-11-03; paper body left unread.
relevance: The draft-then-verify trick that speeds up autoregressive decoding without changing outputs. Decoding K tokens normally takes K serial runs of the model. Speculative decoding uses a small, efficient approximation model to draft several candidate tokens, then runs the large target model once in parallel over those candidates, accepting them under a sampling rule that preserves the target model's output distribution exactly. It needs no retraining or architecture change and works on off-the-shelf models. Demonstrated on T5-XXL, it gives 2 to 3 times acceleration over the standard T5X implementation with identical outputs. The abstract is the record basis; the paper body was not read. DeepMind's independent speculative sampling paper (Chen et al., arXiv:2302.01318) reported 2 to 2.5 times on Chinchilla 70B but was not separately opened.
--- test-time-compute-2024
title: Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters
url: https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314
authors: Charlie Snell, Jaehoon Lee, Kelvin Xu, Aviral Kumar | date: 2024-08-06 | inst: University of California, Berkeley; Google DeepMind
topics: how/inference, how/evaluation, economics/evidence
verification: abstract read 2026-11-03; paper body left unread.
relevance: The scaling law for inference: for a fixed inference budget, how much can extra test-time compute improve a hard prompt, and when does it beat a bigger model? The paper studies two mechanisms, searching against dense process-based verifier reward models, and adaptively updating the model's response distribution at test time. In both cases the best approach depends critically on prompt difficulty, which motivates a compute-optimal strategy that allocates test-time compute adaptively per prompt. That strategy improves test-time compute efficiency by more than 4 times over a best-of-N baseline, and in a FLOPs-matched evaluation, test-time compute lets a smaller model outperform a 14 times larger model on problems where the smaller model already succeeds sometimes. The abstract is the record basis; the paper body was not read.

relevance: The industry-standard yardstick for inference speed, and the reason vendor latency and throughput claims can be compared at all. The MLPerf Inference: Datacenter suite measures how fast systems process inputs and produce results from a trained model, under scenarios defined by a standard load generator with latency constraints, throughput metrics and compliance rules. Each benchmark pairs a dataset with a quality target. Results are split into a Closed division (same reference model, apples-to-apples hardware and framework comparison) and an Open division (different model or retraining allowed), and into availability categories (Available, Preview, Research/Development/Internal). Power submissions measure full-system energy at the wall. The page read describes the suite's structure and rules; no individual system result is recorded because the results tables were left unread.

url: https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/03/how-the-us-public-and-ai-experts-view-artificial-intelligence/
authors: Pew Research Center | date: 2025-04-03 | inst: Pew Research Center
topics: society/attitudes, economics/jobs, governance/oversight
verification: Report page verified at origin; full report left unread
relevance: Pew's paired surveys of 5,410 US adults (12-18 Aug 2024, American Trends Panel, weighted to be representative) and 1,013 US-based AI experts (14 Aug-31 Oct 2024, drawn from authors and presenters at 21 AI conferences, unweighted and representative only of respondents), plus 30 expert interviews. Experts are far more positive: 56% of experts vs 17% of the public expect AI to have a positive effect on the US over 20 years; 47% of experts are more excited than concerned vs 11% of the public, while 51% of the public are more concerned than excited vs 15% of experts. On jobs, 64% of the public expect fewer jobs vs 39% of experts, though both groups see cashiers, journalists, software engineers and factory workers at risk. --- lundgren-tallberg-preferences
title: Governing Artificial Intelligence: Public Preferences and Regulatory Options
url: https://ssrn.com/abstract=7125438
authors: Lundgren & Tallberg | date: 2026-07-16 | inst: SSRN preprint (not peer-reviewed at version checked)
topics: society/attitudes
verification: Abstract verified at origin; full paper left unread
relevance: A conjoint survey experiment in seven countries with diverse political and economic profiles finds citizens strongly support regulating AI and generally prioritize safety over innovation, public governance over private self-regulation, and international over national approaches. The preference for safety is strongest among those who perceive AI as risky, unpredictable, and personally consequential. The authors read this as a systematic misalignment between dominant regulatory approaches and citizen preferences.
