# Why do people disagree about AI's future?

*Because they are arguing about three different things at once: what the evidence shows today, what to value, and how fast the clock runs.*

## The short answer

The disagreement is not noise; it has structure. One layer is evidence about the present. Google DeepMind's own evaluations found that current frontier models show concerning levels of neither scheming (knowingly and covertly pursuing a goal against the developer's intentions) nor situational awareness, though the authors frame that finding inside a proposed safety-case method, not as a guarantee. A second layer is forecasts, which are opinions: 2,778 AI researchers gave a 50% chance to machines outperforming humans at every task by 2047, and between 37.8% and 51.4% gave at least a 10% chance to outcomes as bad as human extinction (Grace et al., 2024). The survey itself warns that its respondents are "experts in AI research, not AI forecasting". A third layer is values and time horizons. Kapoor and Narayanan (2025) argue AI is "normal technology" whose diffusion "occurs over decades, not years", so systemic risks deserve priority over catastrophic ones. Bengio, Hinton and 23 co-authors (2023) answer that risks include "an irreversible loss of human control over autonomous AI systems" and that "AI safety research is lagging". The public disagrees with the experts too: Pew found 56% of AI experts but only 17% of the US public expect AI to benefit the United States over 20 years. AI Now reframes the whole argument as being about power rather than capability. Nothing here settles it; what the sources show is where each side stands and what each can and cannot prove.

## What the sources can teach you

The deepest split is between two framings. [Bengio, Hinton and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17688), in a 25-author consensus paper published in Science, treat rapid progress itself as the danger: the abstract lists "large-scale social harms, malicious uses, and an irreversible loss of human control over autonomous AI systems", notes "a lack of consensus about how exactly such risks arise", and proposes "a comprehensive plan combining technical research and development with proactive, adaptive governance mechanisms". [Kapoor and Narayanan](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) reject the premise. Both utopian and dystopian visions, they write, treat AI as "a separate species, a highly autonomous, potentially superintelligent entity"; superintelligence is "incoherent as usually conceptualized", and a persistent capability-reliability gap separates benchmark scores from real-world performance. For them AI resembles electricity or the internet: transformative, but governed through ordinary means, with systemic risks "far more important than the catastrophic ones". Note what each is: a position paper by eminent researchers, and an author argument by two Princeton researchers. Neither is a measurement of the future.

Forecasts deserve their own caution. [Grace and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843) ran the largest survey of AI researchers to date, 2,778 people who had published in top venues, in the autumn of 2023. The aggregate guesses: a 10% chance of unaided machines outperforming humans in every task by 2027 and 50% by 2047, thirteen years earlier than the same community guessed a year before; full automation of all occupations not reaching 50% likelihood until 2116. On risk, 68.3% thought good outcomes from superhuman AI more likely than bad, yet between 37.8% and 51.4% gave at least a 10% chance to extinction-level outcomes, and respondents even disagreed about whether faster or slower progress would be better. One near-consensus emerged: research on minimising risks should be prioritised more. The authors' framing matters: respondents are experts in AI research, not in forecasting, and their predictions "should not be seen as a reliable guide to objective truth". The survey documents disagreement; it does not resolve it.

Evidence about today's models is the third layer. [Google DeepMind's stealth and situational-awareness evaluations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01420) propose five tests for reasoning about and circumventing oversight and eleven for situational awareness, then run them on frontier models. The empirical finding is that none showed concerning levels of either. Read the framing carefully, though: the results support a proposed "scheming inability safety case", a method with stated assumptions, and the paper comes from the company that builds the models. It is genuine evidence about specific systems at one point in time; it is not proof that future systems will behave the same way, which is exactly what the catastrophic camp says current evaluations cannot establish.

Then the gap between experts and everyone else. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/03/how-the-us-public-and-ai-experts-view-artificial-intelligence/) paired a survey of 5,410 US adults with one of 1,013 US-based AI experts in late 2024. Experts are far more positive: 47% are more excited than concerned against 11% of the public; 51% of the public are more concerned than excited against 15% of experts; 64% of the public but 39% of experts expect AI to lead to fewer jobs. Yet they overlap on control and oversight: 55% of the public and 57% of experts want more control over how AI is used in their lives, and both worry regulation will be too lax rather than excessive. [Stanford HAI's 2025 AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/public-opinion) adds that "the public" is not one thing either: in Ipsos surveys across 26 countries, 83% in China and 80% in Indonesia see AI products as more beneficial than harmful, against 39% in the United States and 36% in the Netherlands.

A final voice changes the subject. [AI Now's 2025 Landscape report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/research/ai-now-2025-landscape-report), "Artificial Power", argues the whole argument is misplaced: "AI is fundamentally about concentration of power in the hands of Big Tech". From this view, AGI talk works as marketing, and the useful question is not whether ChatGPT is useful but whether unaccountable corporate power over AI is good for society. Its prescription, a "zero trust" agenda of bright-line rules, regulating the AI life cycle "nose to tail", and never letting the industry "regulate and evaluate itself", is advocacy from a critical research institute, and its figures arrive through secondary citations.

## Where researchers disagree

Three seams run through everything above. Evidence: the DeepMind evaluations suggest today's models lack dangerous capabilities; the Bengio and Hinton paper argues current evaluations are too weak to establish safety at all. Both can be honestly held because they concern different things, present models versus future assurance. Values: Kapoor and Narayanan rank systemic harms above catastrophic ones, a value judgement dressed as a prioritisation, and Grace's respondents spread their risk probabilities too widely to cluster. Time horizons: the normal-technology view expects diffusion over decades, while the rapid-progress view plans for systems that outrun oversight within years. There is also a dispute about who counts as an expert: Grace's respondents are publishing researchers but not forecasters, Pew's experts are conference authors and presenters, and AI Now says the whole expert framing ignores who holds power. Each side can prove something. The normal-technology camp can point to measured capability-reliability gaps and the slow adoption of past technologies; the catastrophic camp can point to expert probabilities and to AI timelines swinging thirteen years in a single survey cycle. Neither can prove its forecast.

## What remains uncertain

Every forward-looking number here is a stated opinion. Grace's timelines moved thirteen years between the 2022 and 2023 surveys, which is either information or instability in expert opinion, and the paper does not claim to know which. The 2023 fielding predates later capability shifts. Pew's expert sample is unweighted and drawn from conference authors and presenters, so it represents respondents, not all AI experts. HAI's chapter synthesises pollsters such as Ipsos, whose question wording varies by country. DeepMind's evaluations cover specific models at one moment, under stated assumptions. AI Now's numbers arrive via endnoted journalism rather than primary measurement. And no validated method exists anywhere in this library for turning any of this into a probability you should plan around; as Grace's authors put it, the judgments are difficult and there are no established methods of making them well.

## Readings to start with

1. [AI as Normal Technology](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology) — the Kapoor and Narayanan essay (2025), the clearest statement of the ordinary-technology frame.
2. [Managing AI Risks in an Era of Rapid Progress](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17688) — the Bengio, Hinton and colleagues consensus paper (2023, published in Science), the catastrophic-risk frame at its most measured.
3. [Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843) — the 2,778-researcher survey (Grace et al., 2024) that quantifies how wide expert disagreement runs.
4. [Evaluating Frontier Models for Stealth and Situational Awareness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01420) — Google DeepMind's evaluation suite (2025) and proposed safety-case framing for what today's models can and cannot do.
5. [How the U.S. Public and AI Experts View Artificial Intelligence](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/03/how-the-us-public-and-ai-experts-view-artificial-intelligence/) — Pew's paired surveys (2025) showing both the expert-public gap and the common ground.
6. [Public Opinion, 2025 AI Index Report](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/public-opinion) — Stanford HAI's chapter compiling global survey data and the country-by-country divides.
7. [Artificial Power: 2025 Landscape Report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/research/ai-now-2025-landscape-report) — AI Now's advocacy frame, which relocates the disagreement from capability to power.

## Where this sits in the Galaxy

`society/attitudes — Public attitudes`: Pew and HAI measure what people believe, and the belief gaps are part of the disagreement itself.

`how/evaluation — Evaluation and benchmarks`: DeepMind's suite and the capability-reliability gap are where evidence about current systems is made.

`economics/evidence — Evidence and measurement`: forecasts versus measurements is the guide's central distinction, and it applies everywhere in the library.

`governance/oversight — Oversight`: the safety-case debate is a governance argument about who must prove what before deployment.

`economics/ownership — Ownership and power`: AI Now's concentration critique connects the futures debate to who builds and profits from AI.

`politics/international — International politics`: the 26-country divides in HAI's chapter show attitudes are geopolitical, not just personal.
