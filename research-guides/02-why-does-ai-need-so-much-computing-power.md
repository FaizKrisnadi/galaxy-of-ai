# Why does AI need so much computing power?

*Because training means pushing trillions of training examples through billions of parameters on tens of thousands of chips at once, and the machines that answer you afterwards live in warehouses that drink electricity.*

## The short answer

The appetite starts with arithmetic. Meta's Llama 3 paper records that the flagship model was pre-trained on about 15T tokens of text using 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs, almost 50 times more than the largest version of Llama 2, on a cluster of up to 16,000 Nvidia H100 GPUs. Even so, during a 54-day snapshot of training the job was interrupted 466 times, the unexpected ones largely hardware failures. How such systems grow is tracked by Epoch AI, whose survey of over 500 AI supercomputers found leading-system performance doubling roughly every 9 months and projects that, if trends continue, a leading system in June 2030 would need 2 million chips, cost $200 billion and draw 9 GW. Who uses the most compute is a subtler question than who owns it, because the biggest labs largely rent. The physical consequence shows up in electricity: the IEA estimates data centres consumed about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5 per cent of world electricity, and its Base Case scenario, not a forecast, reaches around 945 TWh by 2030. The money follows: Stanford HAI's AI Index reports US private AI investment of $285.9 billion in 2025 and Google reporting more than $150 billion in annual capital expenditure.

## What the sources can teach you

Meta's own [technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783) on the Llama 3 herd documents its own training run, which makes it the clearest single account of what frontier-scale training demands. "We pre-train Llama 3 on a corpus of about 15T multilingual tokens, compared to 1.8T tokens for Llama 2," the authors write, and the flagship 405B-parameter model was "pre-trained using 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs, almost 50× more than the largest version of Llama 2". The hardware section reads like a small city's utilities inventory: "Llama 3 405B is trained on up to 16K H100 GPUs, each running at 700W TDP with 80GB HBM3". Training is synchronous, so "a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job". During a 54-day snapshot the team counted 466 job interruptions, 419 of them unexpected and largely hardware-related. Still, the paper reports "higher than 90% effective training time". This is a company describing its own system, an engineering record rather than marketing.

How the wider trend is measured is Epoch AI's project. The [Trends in AI Supercomputers](https://epoch.ai/publications/trends-in-ai-supercomputers) analysis, published April 2025, curated a dataset of over 500 AI supercomputers from 2019 to 2025 and found that "the computational performance of leading AI supercomputers has doubled every 9 months", with power draw growing 2.0 times per year. Industry's share of the total rose from about 40 per cent in 2019 to 80 per cent in 2025, with the US holding roughly 75 per cent of performance and China about 15 per cent. Extrapolating, the authors project that by June 2030 the leading system would "need 2 million AI chips, cost $200 billion, and require 9 GW of power". That is a projection of current trends, not a plan. The dataset's stated limit matters: it covers an estimated 10-20 per cent of global AI supercomputer performance as of March 2025, unevenly reported, though the authors judge the distribution "broadly representative".

Who actually uses the most AI compute is a different measurement again, and [Epoch's AI Chip Users explorer](https://epoch.ai/latest/introducing-the-ai-chip-users-explorer) is explicit about the distinction: it estimates compute used, not owned. For December 2025, its medians with 90 per cent intervals are OpenAI about 1.74 million H100-equivalents [1.25-2.19 million], Google DeepMind about 1.58 million [1.01-2.55 million], Anthropic about 1.19 million, Meta Superintelligence Labs about 1.00 million and SpaceXAI about 0.62 million. The page states that "OpenAI and Anthropic own very little of the hardware they use", renting instead from Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Oracle and CoreWeave, and that the wide intervals exist because parent-company fleets are not fully attributable to their frontier labs. The estimates are built from power-capacity disclosures, financial filings and third-party analyst estimates, so partly guesswork, though documented.

The physical consequence is electricity, and the [IEA's Energy and AI report](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai) of April 2025 is the reference measurement. "Data centres accounted for around 1.5% of the world's electricity consumption in 2024, or 415 terawatt-hours (TWh)", the executive summary states, with the US at 45 per cent of that, China 25 per cent and Europe 15 per cent. Its Base Case has consumption "set to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030", slightly more than Japan's total electricity consumption today. The labelling matters: this is a scenario, one of four, with Lift-Off reaching over 1,260 TWh, High Efficiency around 800 and Headwinds around 670 by 2030, and a 2035 spread across cases of 700 to 1,700 TWh. The report keeps perspective: data centres still account for less than 10 per cent of the growth in global electricity consumption to 2030, but around 20 per cent of projected data-centre additions are at risk of delay from grid congestion and stretched supply chains for transformers and gas turbines. Scenario projections, not forecasts.

The chips themselves are contested objects, which is why Georgetown's CSET tracks them. [Tracking AI Chips](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/tracking-ai-chips/), from September 2026, opens flatly: "It is indisputable that AI chips are being diverted to unauthorized countries, including China", while "the exact volume of diversion remains unknown". The study evaluates location-verification schemes against four criteria (verifiable, accurate, secure, repeatable) and finds a ping-based system plus targeted physical inspections the most feasible option, tested across more than ten million simulated scenarios in which physical inspections never achieved higher detections-per-cost. Deployment would be feasible but non-trivial: an estimated $3.1-28.8 million a year with owned landmark servers, 0.5 to 7.2 per cent of the US export-control agency's FY2026 budget, and the first of five inherent limitations is that "a PLV system can only detect diversion after it has already occurred".

The money that buys all this is compiled by Stanford HAI's [AI Index Report 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report), a compilation of third-party sources rather than original measurement. Its takeaways report US private AI investment of "$285.9 billion in 2025, more than 23 times the $12.4 billion invested in China", with the report's own caveat that private-investment figures "likely understate China's total AI spending" given state guidance funds, which its economy chapter estimates deployed around $184 billion into AI firms between 2000 and 2023. On infrastructure: "Major cloud providers have accelerated capital expenditures, with Google reporting more than $150 billion in annual capex in 2025", an industry-reported figure. The report counts the US as hosting 5,427 data centres in 2025, more than ten times any other country, while warning that such counts "do not capture differences in facility size, computing capacity, or utilization", and notes that a single company, TSMC, fabricates almost every leading AI chip.

## Where researchers disagree

The sharpest methodological split is extrapolation versus scenario. Epoch projects a June-2030 leading system of 9 GW by extending observed growth rates and says so; the IEA builds four scenarios and frames its 945 TWh Base Case as one possibility among Lift-Off, High Efficiency and Headwinds. The two are different questions, one about the largest single system, one about a global sector, and their numbers should never be merged into a single headline. Ownership is a second disputed framing: the supercomputer analysis measures where machines sit while noting that location does not determine who uses them, since cloud lets anyone rent; the chip-users explorer measures use and finds the biggest users mostly do not own. HAI's investment comparison carries a US-centric frame the report itself flags, and on chip diversion CSET insists the exact volume is unknown, resisting the confident smuggling numbers that circulate elsewhere.

## What remains uncertain

Llama 3's figures are one company's account of its own run, and no independent audit of them exists. Epoch's supercomputer dataset is self-described as covering 10-20 per cent of global performance, with uneven public reporting behind it; its usage estimates rest on disclosures and analyst estimates with intervals wide enough that OpenAI and DeepMind's rankings substantially overlap. The IEA's executive summary stresses uncertainties in adoption speed, hardware efficiency and grid bottlenecks, and its 2030 and 2035 figures are scenarios whose spread widens with every year added. CSET states plainly that the exact volume of chip diversion "remains unknown". HAI's investment figures inherit the caveats of the third-party sources it compiles. What none of these sources can tell you: today's actual energy use per AI query, or whether the 2025 investment surge continues if revenue disappoints.

## Readings to start with

1. [The Llama 3 Herd of Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783) — Meta's technical report, which documents its own training scale: 15T tokens, 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs, 16K GPUs, 466 interruptions.
2. [Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai) — the IEA's scenario report on data-centre electricity, the reference for the 415 TWh and 945 TWh figures.
3. [Trends in AI Supercomputers](https://epoch.ai/publications/trends-in-ai-supercomputers) — Epoch AI's survey of over 500 systems, with its performance-doubling and 2030 projections and stated coverage limits.
4. [Introducing the AI Chip Users explorer](https://epoch.ai/latest/introducing-the-ai-chip-users-explorer) — a research organisation's estimates of compute used rather than owned, with confidence intervals attached.
5. [Tracking AI Chips](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/tracking-ai-chips/) — a CSET policy study on where AI chips are diverted and what location verification would cost.
6. [AI Index Report 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) — Stanford HAI's annual compilation, useful for investment and capital-expenditure figures with their caveats.
