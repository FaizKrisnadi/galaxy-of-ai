# Who controls the chips, the cloud and the data centres?

*No single company or country owns AI's material base, but each layer of the stack has a very short list of firms at its centre.*

## The short answer

Take the layers one at a time. Chips pass through a supply chain spread across many countries: a CSET mapping from 2019 data credits US-headquartered firms with 39 per cent of the chain's value, US allies with another 53 per cent, and China with 6 per cent, strongest in assembly, testing and packaging and weakest in the tools and software for leading-edge chips. The cloud layer has been measured: the UK's Competition and Markets Authority found the market for rented data-centre computing "highly concentrated", with Microsoft and AWS each holding a [30-40] per cent share of supply in 2024 and earning returns "substantially above their cost of capital". The data-centre layer has a subtler answer, because the biggest AI labs largely rent rather than own their machines. And a louder interpretation, argued in a Foreign Affairs essay by a historian and a former Google CEO, holds that controlling "models, chips, infrastructure, and capital" amounts to a new kind of empire. Measured findings and empire stories are different kinds of claims, and this guide keeps them apart.

## What the sources can teach you

The chip layer is a chain of dependencies, not one chokepoint. [The Semiconductor Supply Chain](https://cset.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/The-Semiconductor-Supply-Chain-Issue-Brief-1.pdf), a 2021 issue brief from CSET at Georgetown, maps the chain from design, fabrication and assembly to equipment, materials, design software and core IP. Its executive summary, on 2019 data counted by firm headquarters, gives the US 39 per cent of supply-chain value, US allies such as Japan, the Netherlands, Taiwan and South Korea another 53 per cent, and China 6 per cent, strongest in assembly, testing and packaging and weakest in equipment, design software and core IP. A chip can take over 1,000 production steps crossing borders 70 or more times, so no country controls the whole object. The shares describe headquarters, not factory locations, and the data are 2019.

The cloud layer is the one place a regulator has counted shares directly. In its [final decision](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/cloud-services-market-investigation) of 31 July 2025, the UK Competition and Markets Authority found infrastructure as a service, meaning rented data-centre compute, "highly concentrated": Microsoft and AWS each held [30-40] per cent of supply in 2024, Google [5-10] per cent. The brackets are the CMA's own, and the scope is the UK plus European Economic Area, not the world. Both leaders have earned returns "substantially above their cost of capital" for years; UK customers spent £10.5 billion on cloud in 2024; fewer than 1 per cent of customers switch provider each year, with egress fees "a key commercial barrier". The CMA also found Microsoft's licensing practices reducing competition, and recommended prioritising investigations to designate Microsoft and AWS with strategic market status. That is a recommendation, not a designation.

The data-centre layer is where who-uses matters as much as who-owns. Epoch AI's [AI Chip Users explorer](https://epoch.ai/latest/introducing-the-ai-chip-users-explorer), from September 2026, estimates compute used, not owned, by five frontier developers in H100-equivalents for December 2025, with 90 per cent intervals: OpenAI about 1.74 million [1.25-2.19 million], Google DeepMind about 1.58 million, Anthropic about 1.19 million, Meta Superintelligence Labs about 1.00 million and SpaceXAI about 0.62 million. OpenAI and Anthropic, the page states, own very little of this hardware and predominantly rent it. A policy paper from the [Oxford Martin School](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/governing-through-the-cloud-the-intermediary-role-of-compute-providers-in-ai-regulation) draws the consequence: since frontier AI runs on rented compute through a few providers, governments could make those providers record keepers, verifiers and, in some cases, enforcers of AI rules. Its authors judge basic record keeping and workload classification feasible today, while verifying detailed workload properties is "not currently possible without direct access to customer code and data".

A fourth view reads the layers as imperial control. "[Empires of AI](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/empires-artificial-intelligence-ferguson-klaassen-schmidt)", a Foreign Affairs essay by Niall Ferguson, Lisa Klaassen and Eric Schmidt, the former Google CEO, argues the US and China have built empires over knowledge itself, that "one empire is short of watts; the other of transistors", and that TSMC is "the central node in the entire AI stack". Its figures, like over $600 billion of committed US tech capital spending for 2026 and a roughly $295 billion Chinese state data-centre grid, are secondary citations, and the framing is the authors' interpretation. Underneath, an [ISEAS Perspective](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/ISEAS_Perspective_2025_21.pdf) by Cha Hae Won reports that subsea cables carry over 95 per cent of global data traffic and that, in her compilation of TeleGeography data to December 2024, four suppliers (ASN, NEC, SubCom, HMN Technologies) hold about 98 per cent of the subsea market. Her documented cases include the SeaMeWe-6 contract slipping from China's HMN to America's SubCom after US pressure.

These sources are different genres, and the labels matter. The CMA findings are a regulator's measured conclusions, page-anchored and regional. AI Now's [2025 Landscape report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/research/ai-now-2025-landscape-report) is advocacy: from its opening claim that "AI is fundamentally about concentration of power in the hands of Big Tech" it argues the largest firms pair concentration with federal patronage, citing the Department of Energy's sixteen federal sites flagged for rapid data-centre construction and an April 2025 executive order ramping up coal mining with AI demand as justification. Its numbers arrive through secondary citations. Oxford is a proposal; Foreign Affairs is an argument by essayists.

## Where researchers disagree

The sharpest tension is whether AI has changed cloud competition yet. The CMA's measured finding, from mid-2025 evidence, is that AI is "yet to materially alter the competitive dynamics in cloud services"; AI Now and the essay treat AI as exactly the mechanism that locks in dominance. The questions and geographies differ, one region's current market dynamics versus global political economy, so the labels should not be blended into one verdict. There is also a use-versus-ownership dispute about what control means: the labs with the most capable models mostly rent, while the CMA's findings concern the landlords. And the ISEAS author says the binary US-China frame "oversimplifies the reality on the ground", noting Japan supplies 41 per cent of Southeast Asia's cables.

## What remains uncertain

No source here provides measured cloud shares outside the UK and EEA, so the [30-40] per cent figures cannot be read as global. Even inside that scope, shares are bracketed ranges and exact numbers are confidential. Epoch's intervals are wide and its coverage is five US-linked labs. The 98 per cent cable-supplier figure rests on one author's compilation and is not independently verified. The strategic market status recommendation had not become a designation decision when published. And what actually runs inside any data centre, AI training versus ordinary cloud workloads, is largely unverifiable from outside.

## Readings to start with

1. [Cloud services market investigation: summary of final decision](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/cloud-services-market-investigation) — an official regulator's measured findings on cloud concentration, the closest thing here to hard evidence.
2. [The Semiconductor Supply Chain: Assessing National Competitiveness](https://cset.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/The-Semiconductor-Supply-Chain-Issue-Brief-1.pdf) — a think-tank issue brief mapping which countries lead each chip supply-chain segment, on 2019 data.
3. [Introducing the AI Chip Users explorer](https://epoch.ai/latest/introducing-the-ai-chip-users-explorer) — a research organisation's estimates of compute frontier labs use rather than own, with confidence intervals.
4. [Governing Through the Cloud](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/governing-through-the-cloud-the-intermediary-role-of-compute-providers-in-ai-regulation) — a policy proposal to turn compute providers into AI regulation's record keepers and verifiers.
5. [AI Now 2025 Landscape report](https://ainowinstitute.org/publications/research/ai-now-2025-landscape-report) — an advocacy institute's case that AI concentrates power, to be read as argument rather than measurement.
6. [Empires of AI](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/empires-artificial-intelligence-ferguson-klaassen-schmidt) — a Foreign Affairs essay, by authors including a former Google CEO, framing chips, cloud and capital as imperial chokepoints.
7. [The Struggle for Subsea Cable Supremacy in Southeast Asia](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/ISEAS_Perspective_2025_21.pdf) — an institute perspective on who supplies the region's cables and how rivalry reroutes them.

## Where this sits in the Galaxy

`economics/ownership — Ownership and market power`: the CMA's returns-above-cost-of-capital finding and Epoch's rented-compute estimates bridge owning machines and profiting from them.

`infrastructure/cloud — Cloud and compute services`: the cloud is rented data-centre capacity, and the CMA found it concentrated, sticky and load-bearing for AI development.

`politics/chips — Chips and industrial policy`: CSET's segment map and the essayists' TSMC fixation describe the same hardware from logistics and geopolitical angles.

`infrastructure/connectivity — Cables and connectivity`: subsea cables link data centres to users, supplied by four main firms and contested by two powers.

`infrastructure/centres — Data centres and land`: the sunk capital in data centres that the CMA names as an entry barrier is what makes the buildout visible in places like Johor.

`governance/rules — Rules and oversight`: Oxford's proposal asks whether the documented concentration could be put to regulatory work.
