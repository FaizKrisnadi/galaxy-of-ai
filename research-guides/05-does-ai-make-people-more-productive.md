# Does AI make people and companies more productive?

*The small, controlled experiments mostly say yes for narrow tasks; the big, slow numbers at firm and economy level are younger, thinner, and contested, and one careful trial found people slower while convinced they were faster.*

## The short answer

For narrow, well-defined tasks, the measured answer is largely yes. In a 2023 randomised experiment, 453 college-educated professionals did writing tasks 40% faster with 18% higher output quality when given ChatGPT. In the first large field study, 5,179 customer support agents resolved 14% more issues per hour with an AI assistant, with a 34% gain for novices and almost nothing for the most experienced. In a controlled experiment, recruited developers finished a standard task 55.8% faster with GitHub Copilot. In a field experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, GPT-4 users finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at higher quality, but on one task outside the AI's competence they were 19% less likely to be correct. Then comes the counterweight: a randomised trial with 16 experienced open-source developers found early-2025 AI tools made them 19% slower while they believed they had been about 20% faster. At company level, three randomised trials totalling 4,867 developers found a 26% increase in completed tasks, though each experiment was noisy. At the level of whole economies, solid numbers barely exist yet: the macro estimate examined here measures the pre-generative-AI era, and an Ada Lovelace Institute review finds government-facing estimates "sometimes rest on untested assumptions".

## What the sources can teach you

The 2023 experiments set the pattern. [Noy and Zhang](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37440646/), published in Science, ran a preregistered experiment on midlevel professional writing tasks: time down 40%, quality up 18%, and inequality between workers decreased, since the workers who had been weaker gained more. People who got ChatGPT during the experiment were twice as likely to use it in their real job two weeks later, so exposure to the tool itself drove adoption. [Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) studied the staggered rollout of a generative AI assistant among 5,179 customer support agents: productivity, measured as issues resolved per hour, rose 14% on average, with a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers but minimal effect on experienced ones. Their interpretation, offered as suggestive rather than proven, is that the model spreads the best practices of the most able workers, letting newer staff borrow experience. Customer sentiment and employee retention improved too. The [GitHub Copilot study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) by Peng and colleagues had recruited developers build an HTTP server in JavaScript, and the group with the AI pair programmer finished 55.8% faster; the abstract names no sample size, so none is claimed here.

[Dell'Acqua and colleagues](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700) at Harvard Business School, working with Boston Consulting Group, gave the productivity story its most useful concept: the "jagged technological frontier". In their preregistered experiment, 758 consultants were randomly assigned to no AI, GPT-4, or GPT-4 with a prompt-engineering overview. Across 18 realistic tasks inside the frontier, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks and did them 25.1% faster, at significantly higher quality. But for one complex managerial task deliberately selected outside the frontier, AI users were 19% less likely to produce correct solutions. The same tool, in the same workflow, helps on some tasks and hurts on others, and the boundary is not visible from inside the task.

The sharpest counterweight is the [METR study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) (Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein, 2025). Sixteen experienced open-source developers completed 246 real tasks in mature projects they knew deeply, about five years of prior experience each, with each task randomly assigned to allow or forbid early-2025 AI tools, mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 or 3.7 Sonnet. Before the study the developers forecast a 24% time saving; afterwards they estimated 20%. Measured, allowing AI increased completion time by 19%, the opposite of the 39% and 38% savings forecast by economics and machine-learning experts. The authors tested twenty properties of the setting that could explain the slowdown and conclude it is unlikely to be primarily an experimental artifact. The scope limits matter as much as the result: one population, experienced maintainers on mature codebases with early-2025 tools, not to be generalised to all work. What it does show is that the positive experiments do not automatically transfer, and that self-assessed speedup can be badly wrong; Stanford HAI's own compilation of studies lists this result alongside the positive ones.

At firm level the picture is positive but looser. [Cui and colleagues](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535) report three randomised controlled trials of an AI coding assistant at Microsoft, Accenture and an anonymous Fortune 100 company, run as part of the companies' ordinary business. Each experiment alone is noisy and results vary across them, but combined across 4,867 developers the study finds a 26.08% increase (standard error 10.3%) in completed tasks, with less experienced developers showing higher adoption and larger gains. Output here is counted tasks; the abstract reports no code-quality measure. The [AI Index Report 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) economy chapter, a compilation of third-party sources, sets these numbers in context: 88% of surveyed organizations use AI and generative AI has reached 53% population adoption within three years, yet AI agent deployment remains in single digits across nearly all business functions. As reported by HAI, studies show productivity gains of "14% to 15% in customer support, 26% in software development, and 50% in marketing output", with gains smaller in tasks requiring deeper reasoning and a flagged concern that heavy AI reliance may carry long-term learning penalties.

The aggregate evidence is where confidence runs out. An [IMF working paper](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2026/09/25/artificial-intelligence-and-aggregate-labor-productivity-evidence-from-patent-data-579850) (Eklou, 2026) estimates that AI patenting raised output per worker in OECD countries by 0.8 to 1.2 percent, with possible long-run gains up to 3.8 percent. Read the dates: it uses patent data from 2000 to 2017, which predates generative AI entirely, so it measures the earlier AI-innovation era, not the tools people use today, and its figures are model estimates rather than observed gains. HAI's chapter says it plainly: productivity gains are measurable within narrow tasks, but "the evidence at the macro level remains early and mixed" (it compiles, for instance, a 2.7% US productivity growth figure for 2025 against a 1.4% decade average, plus an executive survey finding widespread adoption but minimal realized gains). The Ada Lovelace Institute's [Measuring Up](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/policy-briefing/measuring-up/) briefing, an institute argument about the UK public sector, shows why the gap persists: one report claims £199 billion in potential savings over five years, another £24 billion a year, the UK government's own estimate was £45 billion a year, and a later version of that same estimate is £23.6 billion. Its conclusion: "a gap between the confidence with which productivity claims are presented and the strength of the evidence behind them", alongside the admission that AI does translate into real gains in specific, well-defined tasks like customer service and coding.

## Where researchers disagree

The disagreements run along four seams. First, which evidence counts: randomised effects on narrow tasks (positive, measured), firm adoption surveys (positive, self-reported), macro statistics (thin, mixed, partly pre-generative-AI), and forecasts (plentiful, unverified). A figure from one layer does not imply the others; 40% faster on writing tasks does not mean 40% faster economies. Second, the studies disagree with each other inside HAI's own compilation: customer support agents 14% faster, open-source developers 19% slower, and the honest reading is that population, task and setting decide the sign, not "AI". METR's authors themselves stress their result covers one population and should not be generalised. Third, the measurement methods are under attack: the Ada Lovelace briefing argues that studies measure first-order effects like time saved rather than outcomes that matter, that task-automation methods using LLMs to assess LLM capabilities are becoming automated government practice despite a circularity problem, and that industry often shapes what gets researched, so findings "should be treated with appropriate scepticism". Fourth, optimists and auditors disagree about trajectory rather than direction: HAI compiles a J-curve hypothesis (adoption costs come before gains) to explain the micro-macro gap, while Ada Lovelace warns that "the incentives to produce optimistic projections are strong". Both can be true.

## What remains uncertain

Whether gains persist. Nearly every experiment here lasts weeks; Ada Lovelace notes time-bound studies rarely follow up, and HAI flags a learning-penalty concern for heavy reliance. Quality is under-measured: the Microsoft trial counts completed tasks, the writing study's quality is rubric-scored, and none of the abstracts reports error rates or downstream value. The macro layer cannot yet confirm or deny the micro layer: the IMF estimate predates generative AI by nine years, the J-curve is a hypothesis, and no source here measures generative AI's effect on any nation's actual productivity growth. METR's sixteen developers, however careful the trial, are one small sample of one kind of work. Firm-level surveys are self-reported, and Cui's three experiments disagree with each other in size. What the UK figures would look like after subtracting costs, what agent deployment does at scale (currently in single digits), and whether novice gains hold up outside laboratories and support desks are all open.

## Readings to start with

1. [Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37440646/) — the peer-reviewed Science paper (Noy and Zhang, 2023) that started the field-experiment wave, headline numbers in the abstract.
2. [Generative AI at Work](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) — the first large-scale field study (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, NBER, later in the Quarterly Journal of Economics), showing novice-weighted gains for 5,179 support agents.
3. [Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700) — the Harvard Business School working paper (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023) that measured both the gains and the failure mode among 758 consultants.
4. [Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) — the METR randomised trial that found a 19% slowdown contradicting every forecast, with scope limits stated by its own authors.
5. [The Effects of Generative AI on High-Skilled Work](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535) — the peer-reviewed Management Science article (Cui et al., 2026) pooling three company-run trials of 4,867 developers.
6. [The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) — the small controlled experiment (Peng et al., 2023) behind the most-quoted speedup figure.
7. [Artificial Intelligence and Aggregate Labor Productivity: Evidence from Patent Data](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2026/09/25/artificial-intelligence-and-aggregate-labor-productivity-evidence-from-patent-data-579850) — the IMF working paper whose 2000-2017 patent window makes it a pre-generative-AI estimate, whatever its headline suggests.
8. [Measuring Up](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/policy-briefing/measuring-up/) — the Ada Lovelace Institute's policy briefing auditing how AI productivity estimates get made and used in UK government decisions.
